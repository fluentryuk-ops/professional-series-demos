Framework 1

The Soft Skills Advantage

~3 min read
FREE · B1–B2 ▾
Why Two Equally Qualified Candidates Get Different Outcomes

It happens more often than most candidates realise. Two people apply for the same role with near-identical qualifications - comparable experience, similar certifications, strong answers on paper.

One gets the offer. The other doesn't.

Ask the hiring manager why, and the answer is rarely about competence. It's usually something closer to: "I just got a better feeling about them."

That "feeling" isn't random, and it isn't unfair bias in the way candidates sometimes assume. It's an interviewer reading soft skills in real time:

  • How someone listens.
  • How they handle being wrong-footed by a question.
  • Whether they make the room feel more at ease or more on edge.

Interviewers rarely score this consciously, but it shapes the decision anyway, because it's a preview of what that person will be like with a client, a difficult colleague, or a bad day.

The Research: What the Research Shows

Leadership IQ, a US workplace research firm, tracked over 20,000 new hires across more than 20 organisations for three years to find out why people who seemed like strong candidates went on to fail in the role. Four attitude-related factors:

Coachability, Emotional Intelligence, Motivation and Temperament accounted for 81% of failures combined. A genuine technical skills gap accounted for just 11%.

Source: Leadership IQ, "Why New Hires Fail" / "Hiring for Attitude" study (Murphy, M.).

What This Looks Like in the Workplace

During a remote client call, a technically strong employee gave a colleague blunt, dismissive feedback in front of the client - phrased in a way that made the client feel small for not understanding a technical point. The manager on the call was visibly unhappy with how it landed. But no formal feedback was ever given to the employee about it. Instead, the manager quietly decided, "he's never getting promoted," and moved on.

The employee kept his job - his technical ability was never in question. But he stopped being put in front of major clients, was left off key projects, and never understood why his career had plateaued. Because no one ever told him.

The Takeaway

This is the part candidates most often miss: soft skills gaps rarely get you fired. They quietly cap what you're trusted with. And because they're so rarely raised directly - most managers avoid the conversation entirely.

Most people never find out that's what's holding them back. They just notice, a few years on, that the promotions and the big accounts went to someone else. This is exactly why interviewers weight this so heavily before you're even hired: they're trying to predict that pattern in advance, before it costs them a client. Everything in the rest of this guide - staying composed under pressure, reading the room on a video call - is about making sure the read an interviewer gets of you is an accurate, confident one.

Framework 4

Remote & Virtual Interview Etiquette

~3 min read
FREE · B1–B2 ▾
🎥 Before the Call: The 10-Minute Tech Check
  • Test your camera, microphone, and internet connection at least 10 minutes before the call - NOT 2.
  • Close unnecessary tabs and applications; they slow your connection and risk a stray notification sound.
  • Silence your phone and desktop notifications completely.
  • Have a backup plan ready - a phone hotspot or the dial-in number - in case your main connection drops.
👀 Camera & Eye Contact
  • Look at the camera lens when you're speaking, not at the interviewer's face on your screen. This is the closest video equivalent to eye contact - it feels unnatural at first, so practise it before the real call.
  • Position your camera at eye level. Looking down into a laptop camera reads as disengaged; looking up reads as distracted.
💡 Lighting, Framing & Background
  • Face a light source - a window or lamp in front of you - rather than sitting with it behind you.
  • Frame yourself from the mid-chest up, with a little space above your head.
  • Keep your background plain and tidy. Use a virtual background only if your connection is reliable enough that it won't glitch.
🖥 Presence & Turn-Taking
  • A small delay is normal on video calls. Pause briefly before responding rather than jumping in - it prevents talking over the interviewer.
  • Use small verbal or visual acknowledgements (a nod, a brief "yes") while listening. Silence reads as disengagement more on camera than it does in person.
  • In panel interviews, mute yourself when you're not speaking, and unmute promptly when it's your turn - a delayed unmute reads as hesitation.
